Wearable ecosystem mapping

A coin-sized sensor, and the whole world it plugs into.

A continuous glucose monitor reads sugar levels every few minutes from a filament under the skin. But the sensor is the smallest part. Around it sits a phone, a cloud, a family, a clinic, an insurer and a regulator — five rings of people and systems that all move when one number changes.

What is actually on the arm

Roughly the size of two stacked coins, worn on the upper arm or abdomen.

  • 1Transmitter. Tiny radio, chip and battery. Converts a faint current into a number every few minutes.
  • 2Adhesive patch. Holds it on through showers, sport and sleep. The part that fails first.
  • 3Filament. A hair-thin wire, ~4 mm deep, coated in an enzyme that reacts with glucose. Not in the blood — in the fluid around the cells.

Data journey

One reading, from under the skin to a decision

This happens 288 times a day, quietly, while someone is asleep.

STEP 01

Glucose meets the enzyme

Sugar in the fluid between the cells reacts with a coating on the filament and produces a faint electrical current — billionths of an amp. More sugar, more current.

Continuous
STEP 02

The transmitter turns it into a number

The chip measures the current, applies the factory calibration burned into the sensor, and stores a value roughly every minute.

Every 1 min
STEP 03

Bluetooth hands it over

A low-power radio pushes readings to whatever is listening within about six metres: the phone, a pump, a watch, a reader. Walk away and the gap appears in the graph.

Every 5 min
STEP 04

Software makes it readable

The app smooths noise, corrects for the 5–15 minute lag behind blood sugar, and draws the line plus a trend arrow — which matters more than the number itself.

Instant
STEP 05

Alarms fire

Below a threshold, above a threshold, falling fast, or predicted to go low in 20 minutes. This is the one moment the system is allowed to interrupt a life.

On threshold
STEP 06

The cloud copies it

The phone forwards readings to the manufacturer's servers. Followers on the other side of the world see the same number about a minute later.

~1 min delay
STEP 07

A pump acts without asking

In a closed-loop setup the reading goes straight into a dosing algorithm that raises or lowers background insulin — no human in the loop for most of the day.

Every 5 min
STEP 08

A clinic reads the pattern

Fourteen days are compressed into one page: time in range, average glucose, how wildly it swings, overnight lows. That page drives the next prescription.

Every 3–6 months
Where it strains

The map is only honest if it shows the breaks

Every arrow on an ecosystem map is also a place it can fail. These are the ones people who wear them actually talk about.

It is always a little late

The sensor reads the fluid around the cells, not blood. During a fast rise or fall it can be 5–15 minutes behind reality — exactly when accuracy matters most.

Physics, not a bug

Adhesive gives up first

Sweat, showers, sport and sleeves peel sensors off days early. A whole side-market of overpatches and tapes exists because of one square of glue.

Cost falls on the wearer

Alarm fatigue

Night-time alerts wake households. People mute them, widen thresholds, or stop wearing the thing — the safety feature quietly becomes the reason it fails.

Design tension

The phone is a weak link

Flat battery, an OS update, a Bluetooth drop, a phone left in another room — and a medical device goes silent without anyone choosing that.

Single point of failure

Who pays decides who wears

Coverage rules vary by country, by insurer and by diagnosis. The same sensor can be free, subsidised, or several hundred a month out of pocket.

Access gap

Whose data is it?

Readings sit on a company's servers. Getting them out, into another app, or into a hospital record depends on an API the wearer does not control.

Lock-in

A disposable computer, fortnightly

Radio, battery and electronics thrown away every 10–15 days, per person, forever. Most cannot be opened, recycled, or posted back.

E-waste

Being watched

Sharing keeps people safe and also puts a parent, partner or coach inside every meal. Teenagers in particular describe it as care and surveillance at once.

Social cost

Compression lows

Sleep on the sensor and pressure squeezes the tissue, so it reports a low that is not happening — and wakes everyone for nothing.

False alarm

Your turn

Map it yourself

Use the five rings as a template for any wearable. Print this page and fill it in, or copy the questions into your notes.

1

Put a person in the middle, not a product

Write who wears it and what they are actually trying to do — not what the device does. Everything else is judged against this.

2

List every part that touches the body

Sensor, adhesive, battery, applicator, packaging. Which of these is disposable, and how often?

3

Draw the arrows, both ways

For each thing on your map, write what flows in and what flows out. Data, money, attention, care, rubbish. Arrows are the map; boxes are just labels.

4

Mark the speed of each ring

Which parts respond in seconds, which in minutes, which in months, which in years? Mismatched speeds are where frustration lives.

5

Find who pays and who profits

The wearer often is not the buyer. Follow the money out to the edge of the map and see whose interests shaped the product.

6

Break one link on purpose

Flat phone battery. Peeled adhesive. Server outage. Insurance refused. Trace what stops working — that is your list of design opportunities.
